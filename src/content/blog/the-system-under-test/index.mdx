## The same agent, right once and wrong once

**The same agent, fluent and grounded in a real document both times, can be right on one turn and wrong on the next, and nothing in the pipeline notices the difference.** Two conversations show how.

An Atlas customer named Sarah asks to move to a faster plan. The agent checks what she is on, confirms the faster plan is one a customer can newly take, tells her the price the catalog computes and what the change would be, asks her to confirm, and on yes, applies the change and hands her a reference number. Every fact correct.

A week later a customer named Daniel asks whether his current plan has a contract he can leave without a fee. The agent answers, with confidence, that his plan is free of any contract and he can cancel any time, and it links the plan page to prove it. He cancels. He is charged an early termination fee. The plan page the agent quoted describes the current offer. Daniel's account is on last year's plan, which carried a term of twelve months, and that fact lives in his account record, not on the page. The answer was faithful to the document it retrieved. It was also false. A false answer about a fee is not like a clumsy sentence. It has a number on it, and that number went to a real person.

If this sounds like something that already happened, it did. In November 2022, Air Canada's website chatbot told a grieving customer he could book at full fare and claim the bereavement discount later. The policy on the very page the bot linked to said the opposite. He was refused the refund, sued, and in February 2024 the British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal held the airline liable for what its chatbot said in [*Moffatt v. Air Canada*, 2024 BCCRT 149](https://www.canlii.org/en/bc/bccrt/doc/2024/2024bccrt149/2024bccrt149.html). The first article in this series opens on that case. Daniel is its broadband twin, with one difference that decides how the rest of this series is built: Daniel's contract was never missing from the system. It was sitting in his account record the whole time, knowable, one read away. That is why an agent built right has a chance of never making his mistake, and it is why so much of what follows is the work of proving whether it does.

Both conversations ran through the same agent. Both would score the same on a naive quality dashboard, because the words were fluent and grounded in a real retrieved document either way. One was right and one was wrong, and nothing in the pipeline knew the difference. Daniel's turn is not a story device: it is a committed test, `tests/test_cold_open.py`, and a case id, `q-cold-open-daniel`, in the labelling queue, and it is the single behavior most of the machinery on this page exists to catch. The series opened by arguing [why a passing eval is not a passing test](/blog/your-evals-are-checks-not-tests/). This page draws the system precisely enough to test, so the rest of the series can stop arguing and start breaking it.

## What does Atlas do?

**Atlas is a single support agent for a broadband provider, built on LangGraph. It does three things: answers plan and policy questions from help documents, reads one customer's own account, and proposes changes to that account. Each is a distinct testing problem: retrieval, a scoped read, and a consequential write.** Every turn arrives carrying a `customer_id` that the runtime put there, never the model and never the message. That one fact will come back over and over, so hold onto it.

Worth naming the simplification before it misleads anyone: there is no sign in flow in the repository. The identity is a value the caller of the graph sets, and the whole point of the design is where that value comes from and where it is not allowed to come from. A real deployment would put a token verification in front of it. Nothing else on this page changes when you do.

A customer can do three things. They are not three flavors of one thing. They are three different testing problems behind the same chat box.

1. **Answer** a plan, policy, or troubleshooting question from the help documents. This is retrieval, and you grade it on whether the answer is grounded and whether it is true, which turn out to be two different questions.
2. **Look up** the customer's own account: usage this month, the current bill, the equipment on file, the open tickets. This is a read against the source of truth, and you grade it on scope and freshness, whether it read the right customer and the current state.
3. **Act**: change the plan, cancel the service, reset the equipment, open a ticket, book an engineer. This is a write, and you grade it on authorization, confirmation, idempotency, and fidelity, because this is where a mistake stops being embarrassing and starts being expensive.

Atlas is one agent with tools. It is not a committee of agents passing messages to each other. That is a design choice, and it is also a testing choice. A design with many agents adds coordination that you then have to test, and it buys little capability that one well scoped agent does not already have. It also adds failure modes that a single agent does not have at all. The OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications, from December 2025, lists two of them directly: insecure communication between agents, and cascading failures down a chain of delegated agents ([OWASP, *Top 10 for Agentic Applications*](https://genai.owasp.org/2025/12/09/owasp-top-10-for-agentic-applications-the-benchmark-for-agentic-security-in-the-age-of-autonomous-ai/)). Atlas has no messages between agents to spoof, and no delegation chain to carry one bad decision further. So the real question is not how many models talk to each other. It is what the one model can reach, and on which turn.

The tradeoff is concrete, and it is a tradeoff in test work. Switch between the two architectures below and watch what each one forces you to test.

## What are the parts, and how does each one break?

**Atlas has nine parts, each named by how it fails rather than how it is wired: the agent core, knowledge retrieval, the account and catalog oracle, the write surface, per-intent binding, the guard, the answer cache, tracing, and the test harness. Each maps to a specific OWASP failure mode and a specific test.** A tenth thing on the old version of this map, the chat front door, is not a part any more, and that is where to start.

### The front door, and the part that is gone

The repository ships no browser and no HTTP app. Saying so on a page whose job is to be accurate matters, because an earlier version of this map drew a chat front door as a component of the system. It is not one. What survives is the two rules that made the front door the interesting surface, and both of them now live somewhere a test can reach.

The first: the account identity, the `customer_id` every downstream call is scoped by, comes from the session, never from the model and never from the message. It travels on a non model `session` channel of the graph state and reaches a tool on a frozen `ToolContext` the runtime builds. The model is not asked whose account this is; it is never given the question.

The second: the model's output is untrusted, and the reply is checked before it ships. `check_render_safe` refuses executable markup, an inline event handler attribute, a `javascript:` URL, and a short list of secret and system prompt markers. There is no renderer in the tree for that check to protect, so what it protects is the contract: an answer carrying any of those never leaves the graph, and a test can prove it without a browser in the loop.

This is not a hypothetical surface. In August 2025, researchers showed that Lenovo's GPT-4 support chatbot, Lena, could be made to emit HTML containing an image tag with a broken source, whose `onerror` handler ran attacker JavaScript and stole the support agent's session cookies the moment the response rendered ([Cybernews](https://cybernews.com/security/lenovo-chatbot-lena-plagued-by-critical-vulnerabilities/)). The chatbot did exactly what it was asked. The interface did exactly what the HTML told it. Neither questioned the other. That is OWASP's LLM01 (prompt injection) chained into LLM05 (improper output handling), and the fix is an old one from web security: treat the model's output as untrusted, and escape or strip anything executable before it reaches a renderer. A chat box is an input you do not control, rendered into an output you cannot trust.

### The agent core

A LangGraph `StateGraph` with six nodes: `agent`, `tools_read`, `pre_action_guard`, `confirm`, `pre_render_guard`, and `refusal`. Edges for the control flow between them, a typed state object threaded through the whole run, and routing that splits answer from read from act. Most of that graph is ordinary, deterministic software, and that is the point of drawing it as a graph: it pins down everything that does not have to be improvised. Exactly one node is nondeterministic, the one that calls the model to decide what to do next. That single node is why naive LLM suites are flaky, and it is why the first thing the harness does is put a gateway in front of the model that records every call and replays it. Same input, same decision, every run. With the model pinned, a red in the suite is a real regression and not random noise.

Six is a number you can hold in your head, which is the argument for keeping it there. Every node is a module level function taking its dependencies as keyword arguments, bound once when the graph is built, so a probe that swaps the product catalog moves the tools, the guard, and the oracle together rather than leaving two of the three describing a universe that no longer exists.

The graph is also where the human gets to stand in the loop. LangGraph's `interrupt()` pauses a run partway through a node, surfaces a payload to the caller, persists the whole state against a `thread_id` through a checkpointer, and waits, indefinitely if it has to, until the run is resumed with a `Command` carrying the human's answer ([LangChain, *Interrupts*](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/interrupts)). That primitive is what the confirmation gate on the write surface is built from, which is the next reason the graph is worth drawing precisely: the pause is a real, inspectable point in the control flow, not a convention the model is trusted to respect.

### Knowledge, the retrieval surface

One tool, `search_knowledge`. Ask a question, get passages back as data rather than as instructions. Behind it is a corpus of 45 chunks rendered from a hand written fact registry, where the renderer emits the character span of every fact it places, so the gold label for *did retrieval find the right passage* is derived by intersecting fact spans with chunk spans rather than typed by a person: 119 fact refs, zero human labelling.

What is behind that tool is smaller than the earlier version of this map drew, and the honest version is more useful. There is no graph arm. There is no dense arm either: the chunk index carries a real 1024 dimension embedding column, and no embedding model is reachable in the offline lane to score a query against it, so the dense side is absent rather than degraded. What ships is one lexical scorer, the share of the query's distinct stemmed terms that occur in the passage. And it is measured, at the depth the runtime actually hands the model:

```bash
uv run python -m evals.measure_retrieval
```

`hit_rate@3` is **0.3468**, Wilson interval 0.2687 to 0.4340, over 124 cases. That is a bad number, and printing it is the easy part. The finding underneath it is worse and far more interesting: `lexical_score` has no inverse document frequency weighting and no length normalization, so a mean of 5.1452 chunks tie at the top score of a query, and an arbitrary sort on `chunk_id` decides which three of them reach the model. Order the gold chunk first inside every tied group and `hit_rate@3` is 0.6774; order it last and it is 0.1774. **The tie break moves the metric more than the retrieval does.** On the query *What is the monthly_price of plan-fiber-500?* seven chunks tie at 0.5000, and six of the seven tie by matching nothing but *is*, *of*, and *the*.

Hold that next to the trap Daniel fell into, because they are different failures. Grounded is not the same as true. An answer can quote a current, correct, well retrieved document faithfully and still be false for this customer, because the document describes the catalog and the truth lives in the account. That is OWASP's LLM09, misinformation, the failure mode where confidently incorrect output gets acted on as fact, and no amount of better retrieval closes it, because retrieval was never the thing that was wrong. A retriever this weak makes the point twice over: fixing the ranking would not have saved Daniel.

The documents are also an attack surface. A help article, or worse, a document a customer pastes into the chat, can carry an instruction the model reads as a command. This is indirect prompt injection, and it has a body count: in September 2025 the ForcedLeak vulnerability in Salesforce's Agentforce planted instructions in a Web-to-Lead description field that the agent later executed, walking CRM data out to an attacker's domain when an employee asked a perfectly innocent question about the lead ([Noma Security](https://noma.security/blog/forcedleak-agent-risks-exposed-in-salesforce-agentforce/)). The newer OWASP Agentic list files the same shape under memory and context poisoning, seeding a retrieval store so it pays out later. Atlas keeps its own version of that attack in `probes/poison.py`: each poisoned document pairs one injected instruction with a canary token, an OWASP id, and the tool the injection reaches for, and the probe drives every one of them through the compiled graph twice, once under a model that obeys the injection and once under a model that treats it as data. Without the obedient arm a green report cannot tell a runtime that blocks from a probe that cannot see. Retrieval is not only a quality surface. It is an attack surface that happens to also answer questions.

### Account and catalog, the oracle

Two read surfaces and one domain module that joins them. The catalog surface has the plans, prices, speeds, caps, and terms, what the provider sells; it is also where a price or an eligibility decision is computed in code rather than left to the model, a thread a later article pulls on hard. The account surface has this customer's reality: the plan they are actually on, usage, bill, equipment, tickets. `oracle.truth_for` reads both together and states what holds for one customer: the plan name, whether they hold a term, how many months, whether they are past the fair use threshold.

That join is the oracle in the testing sense, the source of ground truth a test consults to decide whether an answer was correct, the same word the first article promised would come back. There it was a policy PDF. Here it is a function. Daniel's contract was knowable; it was in the account record the whole time, and the last check before an answer ships is the thing that goes and looks. Two risks define the surface.

Scope: every read must be bound to this customer and reach no other, and the `customer_id` that binds it must come from the session, never from a field the model filled in. In a networked deployment the protocol is on your side: an MCP server is an OAuth 2.1 resource server, the identity arrives in the bearer token on the connection, out of band of any tool schema, and the June 2025 specification explicitly forbids a server from passing that token through to upstream APIs, precisely to close the confused deputy hole ([Model Context Protocol, *Authorization (2025-06-18)*](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/basic/authorization); [Auth0](https://auth0.com/blog/mcp-specs-update-all-about-auth/)). Atlas no longer speaks MCP, and the guarantee did not move an inch, which is a story worth telling on its own and told below. Get this wrong and you get the Asana incident: in June 2025 a flaw in Asana's MCP caching layer let one organization's AI request receive another organization's cached result, project names, financials, M&A notes, for roughly a thousand customers ([Adversa AI](https://adversa.ai/blog/asana-ai-incident-comprehensive-lessons-learned-for-enterprise-security-and-ciso/)). OWASP files scope failures under LLM02 (sensitive information disclosure) and ASI03 (identity and privilege abuse). Freshness is the second risk: the answer must reflect current state, not yesterday's cached copy, which is why a confirmed write drops that customer's cached answers before the next question can be served one from before it. When a later article checks whether an answer is true, this is what it checks against, which is why the oracle is a core part of the system and not a database footnote.

### Actions, the write surface

Five tools, and this is the surface that can change a customer's service: `change_plan`, `open_ticket`, `book_engineer`, `reset_equipment`, `cancel_service`. None of the five changes anything. Each one returns a proposal, and the change is applied by the confirmation node afterwards or not at all. That shape is deliberate: the model can reach for a write, and reaching for it is not the same as performing it, so the interesting question moves from *did the model behave* to *what does the runtime do with a model that did not*. This is the highest consequence surface in the system, and almost everything genuinely hard about testing agents lives on it.

Authorization: can this customer, on this turn, do this thing at all. Confirmation: nothing irreversible happens without an explicit, typed yes, surfaced through the LangGraph `interrupt()` that pauses the graph and waits for the human before the write commits. Idempotency: a call that times out and gets retried must not apply the change twice, the same lesson distributed systems learned decades ago, now with a customer's bill attached, and here the key is minted in the step that proposes the write, before the pause, so the checkpoint holds it and a resume executes the key that was proposed. Fidelity: the action executed is the action requested, which is why the summary the customer confirmed is read back out of the checkpoint exactly as it was stored and never recomposed. A confirmation that describes one thing and executes another is a bug you can only catch if the two can be compared.

The reason to spend the test budget here is that the public failures are not subtle. In April 2026 an AI coding agent at the car rental SaaS company PocketOS deleted the production database and every volume level backup in nine seconds; the freshest recoverable backup was three months old ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/claude-powered-ai-coding-agent-deletes-entire-company-database-in-9-seconds-backups-zapped-after-cursor-tool-powered-by-anthropics-claude-goes-rogue)). In December 2025 Amazon's Kiro assistant, handed a minor task, decided the optimal fix was to delete and recreate a production environment, producing a thirteen hour outage ([AI Incident Database](https://incidentdatabase.ai/cite/1442/)). This is OWASP's LLM06 (excessive agency) and the Agentic list's ASI02 (tool misuse): an agent bending a legitimate tool into a destructive call. A wrong read is an embarrassing answer. A wrong write is a changed account. [The test budget should follow the consequence](/philosophy), and the consequence pools right here.

### Least privilege, drawn per turn

Binding is a dictionary from intent to tool names, and it never had anything to do with the transport. Four intents. A `troubleshooting` turn binds knowledge and the account reads, six of the fifteen tools; a `policy_question` turn binds knowledge and the catalog, five; `account_read` binds nine; only an `action` turn binds a write at all. The turn's intent is classified by keyword rather than by a model call, which keeps the lane reproducible and makes the binding table itself the thing under test, and an intent the classifier does not recognize falls back to knowledge only, the narrowest set, so a change to the classifier widens nothing by accident.

It is enforced twice, and the two halves fail differently on purpose. The model is bound to this turn's schemas and nothing else, so an unreachable capability is absent from the request rather than intercepted after the fact; and the dispatcher refuses the call again when it is handed the turn's intent, which is the check that still holds when a payload was built somewhere else. In a networked deployment this is what scope and step up authorization in the MCP specification are for ([Model Context Protocol, *Authorization (2025-11-25)*](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-11-25/basic/authorization)); in process it is a frozen set and a lookup. Either way an unauthorized action is not denied at call time, it is unreachable, because the tool that would perform it was never on the table for that intent. You cannot misuse a capability you were not handed, and binding per intent is how you decline to hand it over.

### The guard, the membrane

The model is untrusted by default, and the guard is where that assumption is enforced at runtime, for the cases binding cannot decide in advance. It is two ordered lists of checks, both defaults deny, and the lists are data rather than a paragraph of conditionals.

`BATCH_CHECKS` runs before any tool executes, in this order: `reachable`, `single_write`, `scope`, `value_bounds`, `budget`. An unreachable tool, a second write in a turn, an identity the model supplied, a plan id nobody can newly take, or a turn over its call budget stops the whole batch before any of it runs. `RENDER_CHECKS` runs before any answer ships, in this order: `render_safe`, `no_other_customer`, `render_truth`. Unsafe markup or a leaked secret, then another customer's id or name, then the claim itself checked against the account oracle, which is the check that catches a fluent, well retrieved answer that is false for this customer.

The order and the shape matter beyond tidiness: because the lists are the public surface, a grader can run the same checks in the same sequence rather than a restatement of them, and an eval cannot quietly drift more lenient than the runtime it is grading. That is the single most load bearing design decision in the whole harness.

`render_truth` also deserves its limitation stated rather than buried. It is cue based. A phrase like *contract free* or *cancel any time* has to appear before the oracle is consulted at all, so it catches those phrasings and not an arbitrary paraphrase, and not a wrong fee amount. It is a specific rule that fires reliably, not a general truth detector, and pretending otherwise would be exactly the overclaim this series exists to argue against.

Failing closed means the default is no. When a check cannot pass, nothing acts and nothing renders; the turn degrades to a fixed refusal and a clean handoff to a human, and even that refusal runs the two render checks that read nothing but their own arguments, so it cannot leak while explaining itself. Binding decides what the agent can reach. The guard decides what gets through. You want both, because they fail in different directions, and because a membrane that fails closed is how you keep one bad turn from becoming the Agentic list's ASI08, a cascading failure that does not stop on its own.

### The answer cache

It is not a semantic cache. An earlier version of this map called it one and it was never that: there is no embedding and no similarity threshold anywhere in it. It normalizes the question, so casing does not split one question into several entries, and keys the answer by the customer, unless no turn in the whole conversation has touched account data, in which case the owner is a shared generic key. That last condition is sticky for the thread on purpose, because a model that read an account three turns ago can carry it into how it words this one.

Naming it correctly retires one bug class and keeps the other, which is the useful part. With no similarity threshold there is no near miss that serves the neighbouring question's answer, so that whole family of cache bugs is out of scope by construction. The isolation bug is entirely still there for the taking, because it lives in the key. A customer specific answer, *your bill this month is forty pounds*, must be keyed to that customer, or the cache will serve Daniel's bill to Sarah, the Asana failure rebuilt inside your own infrastructure for the sake of performance. The cache is a performance optimization (OWASP's LLM10, unbounded consumption, is the risk it exists to manage) with a confidentiality blast radius (LLM02) the moment the key is wrong. The cache key is the whole game.

### Tracing

Every node opens a span: the turn, the model call, each tool with its arguments and its result, each guard verdict with the check that refused, and each terminal node. Recording them is opt in, and the default is a tracer that does nothing, which is worth knowing before you assume a served process is producing a trace tree. The eval lanes install a recording one, and the decoders that read it back are free functions over any span iterable, so a lane asking *which tools ran, in what order*, *what did the guards decide*, and *did a write land* shares one definition of the answer instead of three.

Spans are ordered by an injected counter and never by a clock, for a reason that only shows up once determinism is real: the hermetic lane freezes time, so every span would carry the same timestamp and tie. You cannot judge an agent's behavior, and you cannot learn anything from a production failure, without a faithful record of what it actually did. Trajectory testing reads from it. Production triage reads from it. You cannot assert on a path you did not record, and you will not record it by wishing.

### The test harness

The machinery that makes all of the above testable, and the place most flaky LLM suites are actually broken. Four pieces. One model gateway, recording and replaying the one nondeterministic node, where an earlier version of this system had three near identical ones; in replay it holds no provider object at all, and a cassette miss raises rather than quietly reaching for the network. A canonical digest that every cassette is keyed by, built from an allowlist of request fields so a framework adding a keyword argument cannot perturb a key. An in memory account store seeded with three customers, Sarah, Daniel, and Priya, which is the smallest world in which a cross customer leak is expressible at all, and where a thousand adversarial plan changes move no real money. And an egress blocker that fails a socket call rather than letting a lane discover its own network dependency in CI.

On top of that sits the suite that gates every merge:

```bash
task test
```

573 tests across 15 files, finishing in about three seconds, no keys and no network. It is the least glamorous part of the system, and the part that decides whether everything else is testable at all.

Read the parts back as a list and something falls out: you have rebuilt the field's two security taxonomies from the bottom up, not because the system was designed against a checklist but because the checklist is just an inventory of how these systems break. The mapping is exact enough to be worth keeping.

## What removing MCP changed, and what it did not

**Atlas used to reach its tools through four MCP servers. It does not any more: about 1,100 lines went, the servers, their four committed schema snapshots, their tests, and the in process plumbing, and not one invariant on this page moved. The identity guarantee in particular survived untouched, because it never came from MCP. It came from a Python closure.** That last sentence is the reason this section exists.

Three things pushed the removal, and each is a specific kind of cost rather than a matter of taste.

**A module existed to patch the framework.** Thirty lines of hardening code reached into private attributes on the tool manager to force `additionalProperties: false` onto every schema, because the server built each schema by introspection and exposed no keyword for it. Writing the schema literal by hand makes that a key in the schema instead of a patch applied after the fact to something you did not control.

**The contract was derived from docstrings.** The tool schema was built from the function's documentation, so reformatting a docstring changed a committed contract snapshot and turned a test red with no change in behavior at all. When the schema is an explicit literal, the contract is the code, and there is nothing left for a reformat to break.

**The protocol boundary was never crossed.** Every tool call was dispatched through MCP's in memory test transport, which runs a client and a server in one process and serializes between them, to call a local Python function. That is a real price in indirection, in error handling, and in the amount of machinery a reader has to hold in their head, paid for a network hop that never happened.

What replaced it is a plain registry: a tool is a name, a description, a hand written JSON Schema, and a function. Fifteen of them, across four surfaces, and no module in the tree imports `mcp` any more. The invariant the whole series leans on is now one assertion you can run in a second:

```bash
uv run python -c "
from agent.tools import REGISTRY
from agent.tools.spec import identity_leaks
print('tools:', len(REGISTRY))
print('identity_leaks:', identity_leaks(REGISTRY.values()))
print('closed schemas:', all(s.parameters['additionalProperties'] is False for s in REGISTRY.values()))
print('every declared property:', sorted({p for s in REGISTRY.values() for p in s.parameters['properties']}))
"
```

```
tools: 15
identity_leaks: ()
closed schemas: True
every declared property: ['plan_id', 'query', 'reason_category', 'slot', 'subject']
```

Fifteen tools, and the only five things the model is ever allowed to fill in are a plan id, a query, a cancellation reason category, a booking slot, and a ticket subject. No schema declares an identity shaped name, and every schema is closed, so an identity cannot arrive as an undeclared extra either. Under MCP that same check was a name sweep across four servers plus four byte identical committed snapshots. The property being enforced never changed; the amount of machinery in the way of reading it did.

What is genuinely given up, named rather than pretended away: *uses MCP* as a line in this series, and a slightly less representative specimen, since production agents often do speak MCP. Against that, every failure mode this series actually grades, tool call correctness, authorization on dispatch, the write guard, the budget, and identity, is preserved exactly. The lesson generalizes past this repository: when a guarantee survives the removal of the component you believed was providing it, you have learned where the guarantee actually lived.

## Follow one turn across every part

**The map is easier to trust once you watch a single turn cross it. Take Sarah's upgrade, the success from the top of the page, and follow it through every part.** Nothing here is improvised; each arrow is a node, an edge, or a guard verdict that the trace records as the run executes.

<TurnTrace />

The turn arrives carrying the identity, and the identity does not come from the words Sarah typed. The core reads her current plan and the catalog through the oracle, both scoped to her `customer_id` by the context object the runtime built, never by an argument the model chose. The turn is classified as an action, so the write surface is bound for this turn and only this turn. The proposal is drafted, the same render ladder that checks an answer checks the sentence she is about to confirm, and then the graph stops: `interrupt()` persists the run, holding the idempotency key that was minted a step earlier, and waits. On *yes*, the run resumes, the stored proposal is executed under that key, her cached answers are dropped because her account just changed, and a reference number comes back. Every read, every tool call and its arguments, the guard's verdicts, and the final reply are spans in the trace.

Atlas is not magic, and that check is a specific cue based rule rather than a truth detector. The honest position is that grounded versus true is the hardest thing in this system to get right, and this is why a whole later article exists for it. But the contract was always one read away. The job of the architecture is to make sure that something actually looks at the account.

## What are the four invariants?

**Four invariants make every test in the series mean something: nothing external is real in CI, the model is recorded and replayed, every turn is traced from the first commit, and the customer's identity comes from the session, never from the model. Pull any one out and the tests stop meaning anything.** They are structural, not polish.

1. **Nothing external is real in CI.** The account and catalog reads run against an in memory store seeded with three customers, and an egress blocker fails any socket call rather than letting a lane quietly acquire a network dependency. A test never depends on a live system, never reads a real person's bill, and never moves real money, which is exactly what makes it safe to attempt a thousand plan changes, and every adversarial variation of them, before lunch.
2. **The model is recorded and replayed.** The one nondeterministic node runs through a gateway that records its calls and replays them, so behavior is deterministic and a failure reproduces on demand. In replay the gateway holds no provider object at all, and a cassette miss raises instead of falling through to the network, which is the difference between a hermetic lane and a lane that is hermetic as long as nobody changes a prompt. A flaky agent test is almost always an unpinned model, not a real bug.
3. **Every turn is traced, from the first commit.** Every node opens a span; recording them is opt in, and the eval lanes turn it on. You bolt tracing on at the start, or you spend the rest of the series unable to assert on what the agent did, because you cannot grade a path you did not record. There is no retrofitting your way out of that.
4. **Identity comes from the session, never the model.** The `customer_id` travels on a non model channel of the graph state and reaches a tool on a frozen context object the runtime builds. It never appears as a field in any tool schema, and the check for that is one command, run above. This is the invariant that survived having its transport removed underneath it, which is the strongest evidence available that it was ever real. The moment whose account this is becomes something the model decides, you have handed an attacker a steering wheel. This is the confused deputy failure the Asana incident is made of, and the cheapest way to lose a customer's data is to let the model choose whose data it is.

## What does each article test?

**[The series](/blog/series/evals-are-checks-not-tests/) moves left to right across this map, roughly in the order you would build the testing: define correctness with a golden dataset, choose metrics, decide what an LLM judge is and is not licensed to say, make the numbers honest with statistics, then test retrieval, trajectory, simulation, and security surface by surface before taking the whole thing to production.**

First, define what correct even means by building a golden dataset, and face the risk hidden in every labeling choice that goes into it, including the discovery that a label you can derive from provenance is a label nobody can quietly get wrong. Then learn to measure it: choose the metrics this system actually needs and write the graders that compute them. Then the judge, and here the series has to be careful about its own claims: an LLM judge is itself a biased model, no human labels exist for Atlas yet, and the judge contract carries an optional calibration record with the rule that a contract without one is unlicensed. What did happen is a selection by measurement, on a small set whose ground truth is true by construction, and the article is about the difference between those two words. Then make the measurement honest with statistics, so a green number survives the question *would this hold on a different sample*, and a regression is a signal rather than noise.

With the ground rules set, the series walks the surfaces. Retrieval: test the knowledge layer against derived gold labels, find out that the tie break moves the score more than the ranking does, and separate grounded from true the whole way. Trajectory: test the agent as a path and not a last message, the tool calls, the order, the confirmation gate, the budgets, on the write surface where a mistake changes an account. Simulation: stop writing conversations by hand and let a simulated user push Atlas through flows that span many turns nobody scripted in advance. Security: turn the documents and the write surface into an adversary's playground, prompt injection, jailbreaks, socially engineered actions, and watch every landed attack become a committed regression.

Then it leaves the lab: take the same instruments to real traffic, watch the numbers drift, and feed real failures back into the dataset, which closes the loop the series opened. And last, the argument that the whole series has been making from the start: architecture is a testing lever. The binding per intent, the guard that fails closed, the identity bound to the session drawn on this page are not just design choices, they are what makes the thing testable at all. You do not test quality into a system. You build it so that testing can find the truth.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does "grounded is not the same as true" mean for a support agent?

Grounded means the answer is faithful to a document the system retrieved. True means the answer agrees with this customer's actual account. The two come apart when the document describes the current catalog and the truth lives in the account record, which is exactly how a faithful, well retrieved answer about a fee can still be false and cost a real person money. Daniel's answer was grounded in the current plan page and false for the plan he was actually on, the same gap that made Air Canada liable for what its chatbot said in [*Moffatt v. Air Canada*, 2024 BCCRT 149](https://www.canlii.org/en/bc/bccrt/doc/2024/2024bccrt149/2024bccrt149.html).

### Why is Atlas one agent instead of a system of many agents?

Because designs with many agents buy coordination complexity you then have to test, and little capability you couldn't get from one well scoped agent. A single agent also sidesteps two of the OWASP Agentic Top 10 risks by construction: insecure communication between agents (ASI07) and most of cascading failures (ASI08) ([OWASP, *Top 10 for Agentic Applications*](https://genai.owasp.org/2025/12/09/owasp-top-10-for-agentic-applications-the-benchmark-for-agentic-security-in-the-age-of-autonomous-ai/)). The interesting question was never how many models talk to each other, but what the one model is allowed to reach, and on which turn.

### How does Atlas stop one customer from seeing another's data?

Every read is scoped to a `customer_id` that comes from the session and reaches a tool on a context object the runtime builds, never as a tool argument the model can fill in. None of the 15 tool schemas declares an identity shaped parameter, and every schema is closed to undeclared extras. The answer cache keys anything touched by account data to that customer so it can never serve one person's bill to another, and the guard refuses to render a reply carrying another customer's id or name. The failure to avoid is the [June 2025 Asana incident](https://adversa.ai/blog/asana-ai-incident-comprehensive-lessons-learned-for-enterprise-security-and-ciso/), where a caching layer crossed the tenant boundary and served one organization's data to another.

### Where does the customer identity come from, and why does it matter?

From the session, never from the model and never from the chat message. It travels on a non model channel of the graph state and reaches a tool on a frozen context object, out of band of every tool schema. In a networked deployment the [MCP Authorization spec (2025-06-18)](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/basic/authorization) makes the same thing the protocol default: identity arrives in the bearer token, not a tool argument. Atlas removed MCP entirely and the guarantee did not change, because it was always a closure and never the transport. The moment whose account this is becomes something the model decides, you have handed an attacker a steering wheel, which is the confused deputy failure behind the [Asana cross tenant incident](https://adversa.ai/blog/asana-ai-incident-comprehensive-lessons-learned-for-enterprise-security-and-ciso/).

### Why fake every external system in CI?

Because a test that depends on a live system reads a real person's bill and can move real money. An in memory account store seeded with three customers lets you attempt a thousand plan changes, and every adversarial variation of them, before lunch, with isolation and scope under test and nothing real at risk. Three is the smallest seed that makes a cross customer leak expressible at all, and an egress blocker fails any socket call so the lane cannot quietly acquire a dependency on something real.

### What is the OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications?

The OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications, published December 2025, catalogs ten failure modes specific to agents that use tools, from goal hijack (ASI01) to cascading failures (ASI08). It is the series' threat model: every part of Atlas is named by the ASI or LLM entry it can fail under, and every entry becomes concrete test cases in the suites.

### How does a confirmation gate stop an agent from making an unauthorized change?

Before any irreversible write, LangGraph's `interrupt()` pauses the run, persists the whole state, and waits for an explicit typed yes from the human before the action commits. The pause is a real point in the control flow, not a convention the model is trusted to honor, so nothing irreversible happens until a person confirms it. In Atlas the idempotency key is minted in the step that proposes the write, before the pause, so a resume executes the key the customer actually confirmed rather than a fresh one, and the summary is read back out of the checkpoint as it was stored rather than recomposed.
