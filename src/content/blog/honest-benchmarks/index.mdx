<Callout variant="info" title="tl;dr">
**A score without a confidence interval is an anecdote: put a 95% interval on every headline eval metric, and gate releases on the interval's lower bound, never the point.** The eval lane produces anecdotes by default, because a number off a sample is a random variable wearing the costume of a fact. This article is the statistics that make the probabilistic machinery honest: a 95% interval on every headline metric, the bootstrap for the scores with no clean formula, an overlap test before anyone calls a winner, and an honest account of what an unpaired comparison costs when two systems saw the same items. The confidence intervals the [metrics article](/blog/the-lying-judge/) deferred land here. It closes on the rule that ties the whole series together: gate on the lower bound of the interval, never the point, so "we cannot tell yet" fails closed instead of shipping on optimism.

## The A/B that refused to call a winner

**Two local models, the same fifty cases, the same graders: both arms passed 50 of 50, both Wilson intervals are 0.9287 to 1.0000, they overlap, and the verdict the study prints is NO CLAIM.**

The A/B finishes and the table is a disappointment in a shape benchmark tables usually are not. Arm A is `ollama:qwen2.5:7b`, the incumbent. Arm B is `ollama:qwen3.5:9b`, the candidate. Both ran the same fifty cases through the same agent, both were recorded through the model gateway and then replayed with every network host blocked, and every verdict came from an executable grader rather than from a judge. Arm A passed 50 of 50. Arm B passed 50 of 50. The point difference is +0.0000, the two intervals are identical, `intervals_overlap` returns `True`, and the study prints the only sentence the evidence supports: `NO CLAIM: the intervals overlap, so this evidence cannot separate the two arms`. Run `uv run python -m evals.ab_study` in the [Atlas repo](https://github.com/mariusargatu/Atlas), the runnable reference system this whole series is built around, and every number in this paragraph prints again, byte for byte.

Saying that out loud, in a room that wanted a winner, is most of the job this article is about. And there is a second finding underneath the first, which is the more useful one: a perfect score on both arms is a statement about the measure before it is a statement about the models. The metric is saturated. Two of the three graders are the runtime's own render guard run a second time, and the guard refuses anything false before it ships, so on an answer that actually reached the customer the grader agrees by construction. This A/B measures the guard, not the model.

The models do differ, and the difference is visible one layer up, in how much work the guard had to do. Arm A needed it on 5 of 50 turns, `[0.0435, 0.2136]`. Arm B needed it on 8 of 50, `[0.0834, 0.2851]`. Those intervals overlap too, so that gap is not claimable either, but the refused sentences are worth reading. Arm B answered the cold open question with "Yes, all our plans are completely contract-free!" to a customer holding a twelve month term, and the guard caught it before it shipped.

The textbook version of this problem is a closer call than a ceiling, and it is the one worth carrying in your head, so work it as a hypothetical. It is invented, not measured: two versions over a hundred items, A at 84 and B at 81.

<BenchmarkVerdict />

Put a 95% confidence interval on each score and they overlap, 75.6 to 89.9 against 72.2 to 87.5. Those two intervals are real arithmetic on invented counts: `wilson_interval(84, 100)` and `wilson_interval(81, 100)` from `evals/stats.py` return exactly them. Now suppose the versions disagreed on eleven of the hundred items, seven that A passed and B failed and four the other way. The exact binomial test on those eleven, hand arithmetic rather than anything the repository runs, returns p = 0.55, more than ten times the usual 0.05 line. The three points sit well inside the noise. There is no regression there to find, only a smaller sample than the question deserved.

This is the line that separates an eval from a vibe. The regression lane never meets this problem, because it is deterministic, replayed, and gated on a hard pass or fail; the same input returns the same verdict forever. The eval lane lives on the opposite ground, on live, sampled, stochastic runs, and a number off a sample is a random variable wearing the costume of a fact. So the eval lane needs a discipline the regression lane does not: every headline metric ships with the uncertainty around it, or it does not ship. A benchmark table whose intervals overlap, with a sentence underneath that says so plainly, is not a weaker result than a confident one. It is the honest one, and the honesty is the signal.

## Which confidence interval fits which eval metric?

**Pick the interval by the metric's shape: a Wilson score interval for pass rates and any proportion, a symmetric Wald or t interval only for the mean of a bounded score at large n, and the bootstrap for anything with no clean formula.**

Once the rule is "no bare point estimates," the question turns mechanical: which interval for which number. The shape of the metric decides it, and getting the shape wrong is its own quiet failure.

A pass rate is a proportion, and the honest interval for a proportion is the [Wilson score interval](https://doi.org/10.2307/2276774), not the textbook one. The textbook interval, plus or minus 1.96 standard errors slapped symmetrically around the point, is fine in the fat middle and a lie at the edges. Quote a 98% pass rate on fifty cases with the symmetric formula and it will hand you an upper bound above 100%, which is not a number a pass rate can hold. [Brown, Cai, and DasGupta](https://doi.org/10.1214/ss/1009213286) called the standard interval's coverage "erratic" even at large n and recommended Wilson's interval in its place (*Statistical Science*, 2001). The Wilson interval keeps its feet inside zero and one where the data actually lives, and it stays honest at small n, where you spend most of your testing life.

For the mean of a bounded score, faithfulness averaged over a batch, helpfulness over a panel, the symmetric interval is allowed back in, but only when the sample is large enough and tame enough for its assumptions to hold. Default to it where it fits, and reach for resampling the moment it does not.

The 1.96 is just the width that brackets the middle 95% of a normal curve, and 95% is the convention worth holding across every dashboard tile, every report row, every release slide. But state what the interval means correctly, because the common gloss is wrong in a way that bites on a release call. A 95% interval is not "a 95% chance the truth sits inside these two numbers." It is a procedure that, run over and over, brackets the truth 95% of the time. The truth is fixed; the interval is the random thing. That distinction sounds pedantic right up until someone bets a release on it. At that point the difference between a claim about the world and a claim about your procedure is the difference between rigor and superstition. Report the interval, and report it as what it is.

## How do you bootstrap a metric with no clean formula?

**When a metric has no closed-form standard error, bootstrap it: resample the per-item results with replacement a thousand times, take the middle 95% of the spread, and always resample at the unit the data is independent on.**

Plenty of the numbers that matter have no clean formula behind them. NDCG over a ranked retrieval, a clustered task success rate, a faithfulness mean whose distribution is nobody's idea of normal: there is no tidy standard error to trust, and forcing one onto them is how you get a confident interval around a number that did not earn it. For everything in that bucket, the bootstrap is the answer, and it is close to magic in how little it assumes. In the Atlas retrieval report those are the ranking metrics, each one a mean of per query scores: `mrr@3` at 0.2527 `[0.1855, 0.3226]`, `ndcg@3` at 0.2111 `[0.1544, 0.2700]`, `map@3` at 0.1696 `[0.1162, 0.2247]`, every interval from 2000 resamples.

The move is humble:

1. Take your set of per item results.
2. Draw a new set of the same size by sampling from it with replacement.
3. Recompute the metric on the resample.
4. Repeat: a thousand resamples for anything high stakes, more when you can afford it, fewer only for a smoke check.

Each resample yields one plausible value of the metric, and the spread of those thousand values is an empirical picture of the metric's uncertainty that never once assumed a bell curve ([Efron and Tibshirani, *An Introduction to the Bootstrap*, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1201/9780429246593)). The middle 95% of that spread is your interval. When the statistic crowds a ceiling, NDCG pinned near one, the bias corrected and accelerated variant is the textbook answer rather than the plain percentile, because a metric pressed against its own boundary needs the correction to keep the interval honest ([Efron, "Better Bootstrap Confidence Intervals," *JASA*, 1987](https://doi.org/10.2307/2289144)). Atlas ships the percentile version only, `bootstrap_ci` in `evals/stats.py`, pure stdlib. BCa was cut on the rebuild and the reasoning is worth repeating, because it is the same reasoning that runs through this whole article: a bias correction plus an acceleration constant plus a jackknife is three more concepts to slightly improve an interval the percentile bootstrap already gives you at this n, and every concept in a reference system has to earn the reader it costs.

One detail carries more weight than all the rest: resample at the unit your data is independent on. For one shot cases that means per item. For a conversation with multiple turns it means per conversation, the whole transcript as the unit, because turns inside one conversation are correlated, and resampling them individually invents precision you do not have. Get the unit wrong and the bootstrap will cheerfully hand you a tight, reproducible, completely fake interval. Atlas has no second estimator for that, and it does not pretend to: the rule lives in the one function it ships, whose docstring says "Resample per query, never per turn: turns are correlated," and the measurement lane only ever hands it per query scores. That is a convention with a comment on it rather than a clustered bootstrap, which is a weaker enforcement than a function, and saying which one you have is the point.

And reproducible it must be, in the literal sense. `bootstrap_ci` takes its seed as a required keyword argument, so there is no unseeded way to call it, and the retrieval report prints the seed in its own header: `bootstrap 2000 percentile resamples, seed 20260727`. The proof is not the design, it is the rerun. `uv run python -m evals.measure_retrieval` piped into `diff` against its own previous output comes back byte identical, and byte identical again when the same run executes with every socket route blocked, which is direct evidence that no number in it came from the network. An interval that shifts every time you compute it is not a measurement. It is weather.

## How do you tell if an A/B model gap is real?

**Two systems tested on the same items call for a paired test: it reads the per-item difference, so item difficulty, the biggest source of variance, cancels and the real signal survives. Atlas ships none of them, deliberately, and pays for it in power rather than in honesty.**

Back to the comparison that started this, because it is the most common one there is: A against B, two models or a feature on and off, and the question of whether the gap is real. The paired test is the single most useful fact in applied eval statistics, and the reason it works is worth seeing in full, including from a codebase that decided not to have one.

The reason is variance, and where it hides. Some items are simply harder than others, and that item to item difficulty is the largest source of spread in most evals. An unpaired test, comparing two independent piles of scores, leaves that difficulty variance sitting in the denominator, drowning the very signal you are hunting. A paired test looks at the per item difference instead, A minus B on each case, and the difficulty cancels, because the hard item was hard for both. What is left is the thing you wanted to measure: did B actually move, item by item. For continuous scores like NDCG or faithfulness, bootstrap or permute that per item difference; for a straight pass or fail per item, [McNemar's test](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02295996) reads only the cases where the two disagreed, which is the only place the answer can come from anyway (Psychometrika, 1947). This is the comparison [Dietterich](https://doi.org/10.1162/089976698300017197) recommended for exactly this job, choosing between two learners on shared data, decades before the agents arrived (*Neural Computation*, 1998).

In the hypothetical above, the versions disagreed on eleven of the hundred items: seven that A passed and B failed, four the other way. McNemar reads only those eleven, splits them against a coin, and returns p = 0.55. The seventy seven both passed and the twelve both failed carry no information about which version is better, and the test correctly ignores them. A net of three discordant items out of a hundred is not a regression. It is the sound a hundred items make.

Now the part that matters more than the technique, because it is the part a reference system can actually be held to. Atlas contains none of this. `evals/stats.py` has no McNemar, no paired bootstrap difference, no permutation test and no power analysis, and no p value is computed anywhere in the repository by any other route. The stance is narrower on purpose: put an interval on every rate, gate on lower bounds, refuse to claim a difference when two intervals overlap. That is one technique instead of four, and anyone can read the verdict off the printed report without trusting a test they did not run.

The cost is power, not honesty, and it is worth pricing exactly rather than waving at. Take the retrieval set, 124 queries, 43 of them hit at k=3. Lift that to 50 of 124, a genuine gain of 5.6 points, and the two Wilson intervals, `[0.2687, 0.4340]` against `[0.3211, 0.4912]`, still overlap, so this method declines to call it an improvement. It would decline even if the seven newly answered queries were a strict superset and nothing at all got worse, because an unpaired comparison cannot see that they are the same queries. A paired analysis would see it immediately. That is the miss, and it is the price of a method that never claims a difference which is not there.

Two warnings travel with this. First, overlapping per system intervals do not prove there is no difference; a paired test can still find one the marginals concealed, which is exactly the miss just described. Second, report the difference interval and an effect size, not a lone p value, because significance without magnitude is its own kind of dishonesty: a gap can be real and far too small to care about, and a p value alone will never tell you which. This is also why the A/B prints its discordant counts as a description rather than as a test: arm A passed and arm B failed on 0 cases, arm B passed and arm A failed on 0. On that data there is not one discordant pair for a paired test to read, so the absent machinery would have found nothing there either.

## How many eval items does the question actually need?

**Size the suite to the smallest effect that would change a decision, not to the calendar. An underpowered eval returns "no difference detectable," not "no difference," and that blind spot ships real regressions behind a green dashboard.**

There is a failure mode upstream of all of this, and it is the quietest one in the discipline: the test that was never big enough to find what you were looking for. Statistical power is the probability that your eval detects an effect that is genuinely there, and it falls out of four things in tension: the size of the effect you care about, the variance in your scores, the number of items, and your tolerance for a false alarm ([Cohen, *Statistical Power Analysis for the Behavioral Sciences*, 1988](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203771587)). Fix the first two as facts about the world and power becomes a statement about n. An eval with too few items does not return "no difference." It returns "no difference detectable here," which is a different sentence, and the whole craft is in not confusing the two.

That confusion is absence of evidence misread as evidence of absence, and it is how a team ships a real regression behind a green dashboard: the gap was there, the suite was too small to see it, and silence got recorded as a pass. [Card and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.06595) found a large share of published NLP comparisons were underpowered to detect the very improvements they claimed, which means the field has shipped this exact mistake at scale ("With Little Power Comes Great Responsibility," EMNLP 2020). An underpowered suite fails by finding nothing, then reads its own blindness as good news. So size the set to the smallest effect that would change a decision. If a two point drop in faithfulness is worth blocking a release, the suite has to be large enough to resolve two points out of the noise, and that is a power calculation, not a round number someone liked the look of.

Atlas does not turn that into a function, and the reason is the same rule the rest of the rebuild runs on. `required_n` and `detectable_effect` existed in the old tree and were cut with the paired tests, and the rule applies to prose exactly as hard as it applies to code: a power claim with no committed computation behind it is an unenforced claim, which is the class of thing this rebuild exists to remove. So there is no figure here for how many cases the A/B would have needed, and there will not be one until something in the repository computes it.

What the shipped statistics can produce is the same problem priced from the other end, and that number is real: at n = 124, a 5.6 point lift is unclaimable. If a gap that size would change a decision, the set is too small for the decision. That sentence is backed by a command anyone can run rather than by a formula nobody ran, which is a worse instrument and an honest one.

And the sizing scales with stakes, because uniform rigor is just waste pointed in one direction. A high stakes surface, anything touching money or safety or the [account on last year's plan](/blog/the-system-under-test/) the whole series is built around, deserves more items, more resamples, and a human reading the borderline cases on top. A weekend chatbot wants twenty cases and a careful read; a thousand resamples on a toy is rigor as theater. Most systems live between those poles, and the skill is placing each surface honestly on the line rather than spending the whole testing budget proving the cheap part works. Rigor is a resource. Spend it where a failure costs something.

## How do you benchmark a stochastic agent?

**A stochastic agent returns a different output each run, so one pass or fail is a single sample. Run k trials per item and report both bars: pass-all-k for anything irreversible, pass-any-k for raw capability.**

Everything so far assumed one score per item. An agent at any temperature above zero breaks that assumption on contact, because the same input yields different outputs on different runs, and a single pass or fail is one sample from a distribution you have not looked at. So the eval lane runs several trials per item and stops pretending the first one was the whole story.

<TrialSpread />

Running the trials surfaces two different variances that a naive average smears together. Within item variance is the model's own stochasticity on a fixed input, the same question answered well on Tuesday and badly on Wednesday. Between item variance is genuine difficulty, some questions harder than others for everyone. Splitting those two apart is a real technique and Atlas does not implement it: the variance decomposition went out with the other cut statistics. Nor could the A/B use it if it were there, because that study records one answer per case per arm, which measures no run to run variance at all.

What the repository does ship is the pair of reliability shapes, `pass_all_k` and `pass_any_k` in `evals/stats.py`, honestly named booleans rather than the unbiased pass@k estimator they are often confused with. They are unused for the same reason: with one recording per case there is no k to read. When you do have the trials, report both, because they answer different questions and a release call needs both. `pass_all_k` asks whether every one of the k trials passed, the strict bar you want on anything irreversible. `pass_any_k` asks whether at least one did, the optimistic bar that measures capability rather than reliability. A task that passes seven times in ten is not the same risk as one that passes ten in ten, and a single run cannot tell those apart; it will hand you either face of the coin and call it the system. Money shaped behavior is judged on `pass_all_k`, because "usually fires correctly" is a sentence that should frighten anyone who reads it slowly.

This is where the two lanes split most cleanly. The regression lane kills trial variance on purpose, by replaying recorded model calls rather than making them, so the deterministic suite is stable byte for byte and a flake is a bug rather than a fact of life. The eval lane cannot kill the variance, because the variance is part of what it is measuring; production runs warm, and the spread is the risk. So the eval lane measures what the regression lane suppresses. One lane removes the randomness to gate a merge; the other quantifies the randomness to tell the truth. Confusing their jobs is how you end up either gating on noise or shipping on a single lucky draw.

## Why doesn't benchmark accuracy generalize?

**Benchmark accuracy is the exact score on your fixed set; generalized accuracy is the inference that it holds on live traffic. The confidence interval covers sampling error only, never distribution shift, so a tight interval on a stale set is precise about the wrong world.**

There is a sleight of hand buried in the phrase "the model scored 85%," and it is one of the most common statistical sins in the field. Two different accuracies hide in that sentence. Benchmark accuracy is the score on your fixed set: exact, real, and a statement about exactly those items and no others. Generalized accuracy is the claim a team actually wants to make, that the system performs this well on the live distribution of things customers will send tomorrow. The first is a measurement. The second is an inference, and reporting the first as if it were the second is the sin.

The confidence interval helps here, but only halfway, and the half it does not cover is the half that bites. The interval quantifies sampling error, the noise from having tested a hundred items instead of all of them. It says nothing about distribution shift, the gap between your set and the real world, and it says nothing about the arbitrary choices inside the system either. The Atlas retrieval number is a live example of the second kind. Its `hit_rate@3` of 0.3468 carries a Wilson interval of `[0.2687, 0.4340]`, and the same measurement under a `doc_id` tie break instead of a `chunk_id` one scores 0.2903, while ordering the gold chunk first inside every tied group would score 0.6774 and ordering it last would score 0.1774. A mean of 5.1452 chunks tie at the top score, so an arbitrary sort key, not the ranking, decides which three passages reach the model. The tie break moves the metric further than the sampling noise does, and no confidence interval on earth would have told you that. [Recht and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10811) rebuilt the ImageNet and CIFAR test sets by the original protocol and watched accuracy drop by up to fifteen points on data that was supposed to be identically distributed, a gap no confidence interval on the old set would ever have warned about (ICML 2019). A tight interval on a benchmark drawn from last year's tickets is a precise answer about last year, and precision about the wrong distribution is just a confident way to be wrong.

So when you report a generalized claim, carry the caveat out loud: this is the score on this set, and the extension to production assumes the set looks like production. Name the assumption rather than let the decimal point imply a coverage it does not have.

And one specific contaminant inflates benchmark accuracy for free. If the model saw your test items during training, and public benchmark questions are the usual culprit, it scores high by memory rather than by skill, and the number is a flattering fiction. [Magar and Schwartz](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.08242) showed models exploit exactly this, scoring measurably higher on contaminated examples than on clean ones drawn from the same distribution (ACL 2022). Decontamination, checking your set against the training data for overlap and pulling the leaks, is what keeps benchmark accuracy from quietly borrowing credit it never earned. It is not exotic machinery either: it is the same overlap arithmetic a [dataset build](/blog/the-golden-dataset/) already runs to drop near identical cases, pointed at the training data instead of at itself. A high score on contaminated data is the most expensive anecdote of all, because it is the one a team trusts most and should trust least.

## The frontier: difficulty, skill, and cheaper truth

**Two frontier techniques: a generalized linear mixed model separates item difficulty from system skill as random effects, and prediction-powered inference fuses a small human-labeled set with a large judge-labeled one into an estimate both unbiased and tighter than the human labels alone.**

Two techniques sit at the edge of what most teams do, and they are where this discipline is heading, so they are worth knowing by name before you need them. Neither is implemented in Atlas, and neither is a thing you can run there. They are named as direction, not as inventory.

The first comes out of the NIST work on evaluating generative AI, which proposes modeling an eval the way a statistician would model any messy experiment, with a generalized linear mixed model ([NIST AI 800-3](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework), 2026). The plain version: a raw average over a matrix of models by judges tangles two questions that deserve to be separated, did the system get better, or did these particular items happen to be easier. A mixed model pulls them apart by treating item difficulty and system skill as separate random effects, leaving the effect of swapping the system as a clean estimate of improvement, net of which items the sample happened to draw. It is heavier than a bootstrap, so you reserve it for the release grade matrix rather than every dashboard tile. And when the matrix runs many comparisons at once, correct for multiplicity before calling any single cell significant, because run enough comparisons and noise will hand you a winner by chance alone.

The second is the bridge from the [metrics article](/blog/the-lying-judge/), and it is the one that pays for itself fastest. Human labels are scarce and expensive; a calibrated judge is cheap and slightly biased. Prediction powered inference combines a small set of human labels with a large set of judge labels into a single estimate that is both provably unbiased, because the human labels correct the judge's systematic tilt, and tighter than the human labels alone could buy ([Angelopoulos et al., *Science*, 2023](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adi6000); the tuned [PPI++](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01453) variant when the judge tracks the human closely). The relationship to calibration is exact and worth stating: calibration measures how far the judge can be trusted, and prediction powered inference is how you spend that trust, converting it into precision without letting the judge's bias leak back into the headline number. Calibration tells you the judge is good enough; this is how you cash it in. The frontier is not more confident numbers. It is more honest ones, with the reasons for the confidence made explicit.

## Where this lives

**When a release gates on an eval metric, gate on the lower bound of the confidence interval, never the point estimate. On real data the retrieval gate lands exactly there: a point of 0.3468 clears a 0.300 bar, a lower bound of 0.2687 does not, and the verdict is FAIL.**

On the Atlas map, all of this lives in the eval lane, the probabilistic machinery that scores each surface and tracks the result over time, deliberately apart from the regression lane that gates merges on deterministic pass or fail. The eval lane does not hard gate a merge the way regression does, but releases still turn on its numbers, and that is where the single most important rule in this article lands.

<GatingRule />

The diagram continues the hypothetical from the top of the article: a candidate whose point, 0.81, clears a 0.80 release bar while its Wilson floor, 0.722, does not. The real one is less tidy and makes the case better, because nothing about it was arranged. Retrieval on the committed corpus scores a `hit_rate@3` of 0.3468 against a bar of 0.300. The point clears by nearly five points. The lower bound is 0.2687 and does not clear. The gate fails closed, and the printed reason is `lower bound 0.269 is below the 0.300 bar: the floor has not cleared, so the gate fails closed`. Shipping on the point there would be shipping on optimism, which is the entire argument for gating on the floor.

In the Atlas repo this is not advice, it is a function: `gate_on_lower_bound` in `evals/gate.py` returns one of three verdicts, pass when the floor clears, fail closed when it does not, and quarantine when the interval is wider than the decision can tolerate. That third verdict is the variance budget made executable, and the same retrieval number triggers it: at a 0.20 budget the interval is narrow enough to judge and the answer is FAIL, while at a 0.10 budget the width of 0.165 exceeds the budget and the verdict becomes QUARANTINE, too wide to call, rerun with more items. An interval wider than the budget is an unproven claim whichever side of the bar it sits on, and a result too wide to call gets rerun instead of shipped as a coin flip. The same function runs over the A/B, where it says PASS on both arms against a 0.80 bar, floor 0.9287, and note precisely what that does and does not say: the pass rate's floor clears the bar on this case set, and the comparison between the arms still refuses to name a winner.

The machinery underneath is built to make all of this cheap and reproducible. `task measure` replays the committed cassettes and prints every measured number in this article; nothing in that lane calls a model, and the report reproduces byte for byte with every network host blocked. The pieces hand off to the rest of the series with two honest gaps named out loud. The [metrics article](/blog/the-lying-judge/) deferred its intervals to this one, agreement with a human included: `cohen_kappa_interval` sits in the same module as everything else here, and it is unused, because there are no human labels in the repository to compute a kappa from. No calibration record exists either, so nothing there is licensed to call a judge calibrated, and that absence is stated rather than papered over. The retrieval numbers this article gates are first stage lexical retrieval only, because no reranker ran in the measurement lane, and a comparison that was never run is not a lift anyone gets to report.

There is one last number worth pointing at. [Daniel](/blog/the-system-under-test/), the customer on last year's plan, was handed a fluent, grounded, and false answer, caught only by correctness against the account and catalog rather than faithfulness to a page. That correctness check is the `answer_true` grader, and it passed 50 of 50 in both arms of the A/B. The honest reading of that is not that the agent is perfect. It is that the guard refused every false sentence before it shipped, five times in arm A and eight in arm B, and the grader confirmed the refusal rather than discovering the problem. A number at its ceiling is telling you about the thing standing in front of it.

<Callout variant="takeaway" title="Key takeaway">
Put a bar on every headline number, and pick the bar by the shape of the metric: Wilson for a rate, the bootstrap for anything with no clean formula, resampled at the unit the data is independent on and seeded so it reproduces. When two systems saw the same items, a paired test subtracts out the item difficulty that hides the signal, and if you do not have one, say so and say what it costs, because an unpaired comparison misses real improvements rather than inventing false ones. Size the set to the smallest effect that would change a decision. And when a release turns on a tracked metric, gate on the lower bound of its interval, never the point, so "we cannot tell yet" fails closed.
</Callout>

The point is the marketing. The interval is the honesty. A benchmark that reports both, and gates on the cautious one, is a benchmark that holds up.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does an eval score need a confidence interval?

Because a number off a sample is a random variable, not a fact. The same suite run twice returns two different scores from an unchanged system, so a bare point estimate hides whether a gap is signal or noise. A 95% interval states the uncertainty around the number, and reporting it is what separates an eval from a vibe. A benchmark whose intervals overlap, said so plainly, is the honest result, not the weak one.

### What is a paired test and why does it matter for A/B model comparison?

When version A and version B run on the same items, the comparison is paired, and a paired test reads the per item difference instead of two independent piles of scores. Item difficulty, the largest source of spread in most evals, cancels because the hard item was hard for both versions. That subtracts out the noise hiding the signal, and on a close call it can turn "we cannot tell" into a clean verdict the marginal intervals could never reach. Use a paired bootstrap or permutation test for continuous scores and McNemar for pass/fail. The reference system behind this series ships none of them on purpose, and states the cost: an unpaired comparison is conservative, so it misses real improvements rather than inventing false ones.

### Should you gate a release on the point estimate or the confidence interval?

Gate on the lower bound of the interval, never the point. The reference system's retrieval gate lands exactly there on real data: hit_rate@3 of 0.3468 clears a 0.300 bar, its Wilson lower bound of 0.2687 does not, and the verdict is FAIL. A best guess above the line and an honest floor below it, and shipping on the best guess is shipping on optimism. Gating on the floor makes "we cannot tell yet" fail closed, which is the only safe direction for a number that might be noise.

### What is the difference between benchmark accuracy and generalized accuracy?

Benchmark accuracy is the score on your fixed set, exact and real and a statement about exactly those items. Generalized accuracy is the claim a team actually wants, that the system performs this well on the live distribution of what customers send tomorrow. The first is a measurement; the second is an inference. The confidence interval covers sampling error but says nothing about distribution shift, so a tight interval on a stale set is a precise answer about the wrong distribution.

### How many items does an LLM eval need?

Size the suite to the smallest effect that would change a decision, not to a round number someone liked. The cost of getting it wrong is legible at any n: on the reference system's 124 query retrieval set, lifting hits from 43 to 50, a real gain of 5.6 points, still leaves the two Wilson intervals overlapping, so the honest report declines to call it an improvement. An underpowered eval returns "no difference detectable," which is not "no difference."

### What is the difference between pass@k and pass-all-k?

Run a stochastic agent k times per item and two bars emerge. Pass-any-k, often written pass@k, asks whether at least one of the k trials passed and measures capability. Pass-all-k asks whether every trial passed and measures reliability. A task passing seven of ten clears the first and fails the second, and anything irreversible, money above all, is judged on pass-all-k.
